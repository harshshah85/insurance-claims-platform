L1 & L2 Architecture Diagrams

System context, container diagrams & a phased rollout — the platform from the outside in, then opened up into its runtime pieces.

Prepared by Harsh Shah  |  June 2026
Two levels of the C4 model. L1 (System Context) shows the platform as a single box, with the people and systems around it. L2 (Container) opens that box into its runtime pieces: the Kafka backbone, the 9 bounded-context services, the agent workers, the read models, and the serving APIs. The lower C4 levels (component, code) are out of scope on purpose — this is a reference architecture, not an implementation. One thing to read off both diagrams: the agent workers are first-class containers that read facts and draft recommendations, but a human (or the deterministic auto-ticket lane) holds the decision. Agents reason, humans decide, and that's built in rather than bolted on.

3 Rollout — A Thin Slice First

Sequenced by what unlocks what, not by the calendar. How long each step takes depends on the organization; the order is what matters.

Stage A — Walking skeleton

One claim type — the Life death claim — happy path, end to end: first notice of loss intake → the event-sourced claim → a rule-based decision → payment, with client/advisor status read models. Thin but complete. This is the step that proves the backbone is real and de-risks everything after it.

Stage B — Breadth

Add DI claims (recurring benefit, recertification, offsets), the rest of the 9 contexts, the full money-movement saga, and omnichannel correspondence with preference/consent. Now the platform covers both lines and the regulated, hard parts.

Stage C — Automation depth

Push the auto-ticket rate up by widening the low-touch path, add rule-based fraud scoring + investigations routing, and harden operations. The value here is fewer manual touches and less leakage — all still with explainable rules.

Stage D — Intelligence (when earned)

Add ML for fraud and STP soft-factors once there is enough labeled claims history to train on — typically a stretch of real operations, not a date on a plan. The same stage introduces the LLM / agent assist layer: models read documents, reconcile facts, and draft a recommended decision with cited detail for a human to approve — and only graduate to deciding clean claims once proven against human reviewers. Hard guardrails (contestability, OFAC, limits) stay as rules, and a human stays the decision-maker until the model has earned more. This stage is optional and gated on data and proof, never on a calendar. (See AI-Assisted & Agentic Decisioning.)

Stage E — Migration & legacy coexistence (// TODO — unsolved)

Everything above assumes greenfield. The honest enterprise version starts from a strangler: route every new claim here, leave the old system running its existing book, and deliberately don't bulk-migrate the back catalog — closed and historical claims age out and die in legacy. The open questions this reference architecture doesn't yet answer: the work-in-progress claims at cutover (drain-in-place vs. synthetic-replay into the log — and a DI claim can stay open for years, so draining can keep legacy alive far longer than you'd like); how the two stay consistent during the dual-run (system-of-record per claim, reconciliation, and statutory prompt-pay clocks that must not pause because a claim straddles two systems); and a read-bridge back to legacy, since a new claim can still reference an old one. See the home page's Open Questions for the full enterprise list.

Why this order

PrincipleRationale
Backbone before featuresEvery later capability is just another consumer of the event log — the backbone is the leverage point
Life before DIThe death claim pays once and is simpler; it proves the platform before DI's long-running complexity (recertification, offsets) is added
Rules before MLExplainable from day one for regulators; ML needs labeled history that doesn't exist at launch
A thin slice before breadthOne claim type all the way through finds the real integration problems early, while the blast radius is still small
