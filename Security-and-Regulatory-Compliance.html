Security & Regulatory Compliance

Claims hold some of the most sensitive details of a person's life, so this is where the platform earns its trust. A 7-layer security design and the full Life & Disability regulatory map — privacy, prompt-pay, and audit treated as features, not afterthoughts.

Prepared by Harsh Shah  |  June 2026
Security and regulation are really one job here. This platform moves money to grieving beneficiaries and disabled claimants using their most sensitive data. The first half below is the technical security stack, edge to data; the second is the regulatory map specific to life and disability claims. The thing that ties them together: the event-sourced log is itself a security asset — it's the immutable audit trail regulators keep asking for.

1 Seven-Layer Security Design

Layer 1 — Edge & Network

WAF on all public ingress (intake, portals, APIs); rate limiting and bot defense on first notice of loss endpoints; private networking between services; no broker exposed publicly. DDoS protection on channel endpoints.

Layer 2 — Transport

TLS 1.2+ everywhere. Kafka SASL/SSL with mTLS between services and brokers; client certificates per service identity. No plaintext on the wire, internal or external.

Layer 3 — Identity & Access

OAuth 2.0 / OIDC per consumer type: clients (portal, step-up auth for payment changes), advisors/reps (workforce SSO + MFA), service-to-service (client-credentials, short-lived tokens). RBAC + attribute-based rules; least privilege per topic and per read model.

Layer 4 — Application

Input validation and schema enforcement at every boundary; signed commands; idempotency keys on money-movement commands; dual control on disbursement; segregation of duties between adjudication and payment approval.

Layer 5 — Data

Field-level encryption of PII/PHI; SSN and medical detail tokenized at the edge — events carry tokens, the access-controlled vault resolves them. Encryption at rest for topics, read models, and documents. Keys held and rotated in a managed key-management service.

Layer 6 — Topic & Stream Authorization

Kafka ACLs per principal: producers write only their context's topics, consumers subscribe only to what they need. Data-classification tags on topics (e.g. PHI on party.identity, di.benefit) drive masking in non-prod and in analytics.

Layer 7 — Audit & Detection

The event log is the immutable audit record. Plus: access logging on the token vault, anomaly detection (bulk reads, off-hours access), SIEM correlation, and alerting on money-movement and PHI access patterns.

AI / agent-specific threats

The agent workers ingest untrusted claimant content (uploaded documents, free text) and call an external model — a threat surface the classic STRIDE table above doesn't cover. The unifying defense is the architecture itself: the model is advisory by design and holds no authority over the decision or the money.

Why event sourcing helps compliance: a market-conduct examiner asking "show me every action on this claim and who took it, in order" gets a direct answer — the ordered event log is that record, with approver identities and causation links. There is no reconstructing history from mutable rows and scattered logs.
The same log makes AI defensible. Every LLM recommendation is itself an event on claims.ai.v1 carrying the model version, the inputs it saw, its output, and its confidence — so "what did the AI suggest, and did a human decide?" is answerable for any claim. The model sees tokenized PII only (same layer-5 controls); prompts and outputs are logged without raw PII/PHI. Because a human (or the deterministic auto-ticket lane) owns every authoritative decision and no denial is ever model-only, an AI-influenced outcome is always traceable to an accountable decision-maker.

5 Data Lifecycle & the Right to Erasure

The hardest problem in event sourcing on regulated personal data: the log is append-only and never deleted, yet privacy law says a human can ask to be erased. The two are reconciled with crypto-shredding.

The tension, stated plainly. The event log is deliberately immutable and tamper-evident — that is what makes it a defensible audit record. But privacy regimes (and some state insurance rules) give a data subject a right to have their personal data erased. You cannot honour an erasure request by rewriting or deleting events without destroying the very audit guarantees regulators rely on. So you don't.

Crypto-shredding — erase the key, not the log

Personal data is never written into events in the clear. Building on the Layer-5 controls, each data subject's personal fields are encrypted with a per-subject data key held in the access-controlled vault; the event carries only the ciphertext (or a token that resolves to it). To honour an erasure request, the platform destroys that subject's key. The events stay exactly where they were — same offsets, same ordering, same hash chain and causation links — but the personal data inside them is now unrecoverable ciphertext. The audit record remains complete and tamper-evident; the person is, in any practical sense, erased.

ConcernHow it's handled
What gets shreddedOnly personal data (the per-subject encrypted fields / vault-resolved tokens). Non-personal facts — that a decision happened, when, the amount band, approver role — survive for audit.
What stays intactEvent offsets, ordering, hash chain, correlation/causation links. A market-conduct examiner can still see that the claim was handled correctly and by whom (by role), just not the erased person's identifying detail.
Retention vs. erasureStatutory insurance retention (often ~7 years) sets the earliest an erasure may be honoured; a request is queued and executed when retention lapses. A legal hold suspends it entirely.
Read models & backupsRead models are projections — drop and rebuild, and the shredded fields simply never re-materialise. Key destruction also renders the subject's data in older encrypted backups unreadable as those age out.
Scope of a keyKeys are per data subject (claimant, beneficiary, payee), not per claim — so erasing one party doesn't affect others on a shared claim.
Net: the platform satisfies an erasure request and keeps an immutable, tamper-evident audit log — the two stop being in conflict because erasure acts on the key, never on the log.
